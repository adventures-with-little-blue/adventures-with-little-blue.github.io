# Summary of the trip

In this post I have no new trip to offer, nor any tinkering. I just thought I would summarise some things about the recent trip to the southeast.

<!--more-->

## Route, distance and timetable

I had planned a detailed route and an approximate timetable for the trip. We followed that route almost 100% – in total, we rode 2,903 km according to Little Blue's odometer.

* day 1: 879 km
* day 2: 628 km
* day 3: 559 km
* day 4: 837 km

I am quite pleased with the route. It was optimised both to pass through the centre of every municipality and to prioritise smaller (and hopefully more enjoyable) surfaced roads over main roads. We did ride a few dozen kilometres of motorway, between Kotka and Porvoo and around Mikkeli. But much of the trip was on three- and four-digit roads – in other words, small roads. There are a couple of route choices I regret. One is the road between Ristiina and Puumala. I chose a gravel road from Ristiina up to Anttola instead of the surfaced archipelago road to Hurissalo (which would have meant missing Anttola). The gravel road ran solely through forest and was difficult to ride, with lots of loose rolling gravel and many bends. The other road would certainly have been much more enjoyable and scenic, and Anttola was not really that spectacular.

The other less successful road choice was from Puumala up to Sulkava. Instead of the shortest road (straight north from Puumala), I chose to cross Puumala Bridge and turn north after that. There I chose the road via the Kietävälä ferry. This road was rather dull and unremarkable, and the surface was also in poor condition. Although it would have been slightly longer and slower, it would have been more enjoyable to take the Rongonsalmi ferry and follow the Immola–Sulkava road (road 438) instead. Along that road there is more water, a canal and, above all, Vekaransalmi Bridge. That road would surely have been in better condition too.

![Route](rutt.webp)

The condition of the roads was perhaps a little surprising overall: many of the medium-small and small roads were in poor or very poor condition. For example, every single road along the first day's extra route (Heinävesi – Tuusniemi – Kaavi – Polvijärvi – Outokumpu) displayed a sign warning of damaged road surfaces. I had thought the roads in Ostrobothnia were particularly bad, but there are certainly dreadful roads elsewhere too.

On Monday I set off at 6 in the morning and was back in Kolovesi from my extra trip at 23:15 – so it was almost 17.5 hours. Of course, that included some breaks out of the saddle. The other days, too, took around 15 hours from start to finish. That leaves around 7 hours for maintenance and rest. This is a pace that works for a few days but is definitely not sustainable in the long run. More rest is needed – sleep, quite simply – as well as more time for maintenance and taking it easy. Moreover, it only works when one is alone. When there are several people, there is always some extra time spent waiting for others and so on.

The main aim of this trip was, of course, to ride a motorcycle, and then to visit the municipalities of south-eastern Finland. The actual sightseeing came third. But despite that, I had planned far too tight a timetable. I had, admittedly, planned opportunities to shorten every day (except the last one), but once in the saddle I could not resist riding every kilometre. It created a bit of time pressure, which led me to cut short the visits to the towns and the time out of the saddle, and to skip most of the planned restaurant visits. At the overnight stops, it was mostly a matter of sleeping; I scarcely had time to look around.

Feeling time pressure meant that I did not have time to enjoy “just being”. I appreciate strolling around a beautiful town, taking a short hike in a national park or enjoying good food, but there was less of that now. Next time, perhaps I should plan less riding and more time for the visits.

### New municipalities

In total, we visited 46 new municipalities in four days. Little Blue and I have now visited 207 of Finland's 308 municipalities together.

### Fuel and costs

I refuelled 11 times (including before the trip), for a total of 124.59 l costing €261.73. The cheapest was at the ABC automatic station on Mannerheiminkatu in Porvoo, at €1.884/l, and the most expensive was €2.199/l at ABC Viipurinportti in Lappeenranta. Average consumption was around 4.15 l/100 km, which must be said to be very good. However, I am a little unsure whether this is actually correct. For one thing, I measured the lowest consumption per tank I have ever had (3.69 l/100 km – which can hardly be right), and for another, the trip meter and the ordinary kilometre meter have suddenly started showing different distances, which suggests that there is something wrong with the meter.

Apart from fuel, I have been fairly frugal. One visit to a fine-dining restaurant and a couple of visits to lunch restaurants. Some groceries from a shop and a coffee from a petrol station. Admission to Olavinlinna and the Emperor's Fishing Lodge also cost a few tens of euros. Perhaps I ought to get a Museum Card?

## Choosing campsites

* First night: Kolovesi National Park (the tent site was 250 m from the car park).
* second night: Päihäniemi outdoor recreation area
* third night: the gravel area in front of a mobile phone mast near Repovesi National Park

I like the idea of visiting national parks by motorcycle, but unfortunately it has proved a little troublesome. It is very rarely possible to ride the motorcycle all the way to the tent site; hiking is usually required. This means that the panniers and other things must be removed from the bike, and everything must be carried a few hundred metres or perhaps a kilometre. If nothing else, this takes quite a lot of time. It is much more convenient to have the bike and the luggage beside the tent. Nor does one know whether the motorcycle will be left in peace over there in the car park, although national parks are hardly thieves' number-one place.

On the second night I pitched the tent in the Päihäniemi outdoor recreation area among other tents, motorhomes and ordinary cars used for overnight stays. The place was beautiful; there was a dry toilet, a fire site and a beach. I parked the bike beside the tent. Otherwise it worked well, but the neighbour in the motorhome watched football half the night, and it could be heard rather well in my tent. Well, I then got up at 6, started the bike and left at 7 – I can imagine my neighbours did not entirely appreciate that wake-up call either. Originally, I had intended to stay at the campsite in Lappeenranta, but changed the plan at the last minute. The experience would probably have been roughly the same – some disturbing sounds at night.

I had planned to spend the third night in Repovesi National Park, but changed my mind because of the aforementioned problem with the distance between the car park and the tent site (in Repovesi, it would have been almost a kilometre's hike). I also wanted to get away early the next day, so making several trips with luggage between the tent site and the motorcycle was not appealing. As a backup place, I had chosen Elfvings tower, only a short distance from the national park. Unfortunately, this car park was already occupied by a motorhome. I then looked at a place beside a forest road, but concluded that it was both uneven and difficult for using the gas stove safely. I found a new place where, according to the map, there should have been a lean-to shelter. Once there, it turned out that what looked like a lean-to shelter on the map was only the beginning of the path leading to it. The shelter itself was 1 km into the forest.

It was now getting late and I was hungry enough to begin getting a little desperate. Next to the place where the lean-to shelter should have been was a mobile phone mast with a small gravel area in front of it. I decided to pitch the tent there. It was not particularly beautiful, but the ground was level and it was also possible to arrange a place for the gas stove (on top of a small flat stone). I also managed to place the tent and motorcycle so that they were scarcely visible at all from the adjacent gravel road. A gravel area by a phone mast is not exactly cosy, but it was quick to set up camp and cook dinner. And in any case, it was time to crawl into the sleeping bag.

![Motorcycle and tent](mc_talt.jpg "The motorcycle parked beside the tent.")


I do not really know how I should choose campsites in the future either. It is very difficult to find that ideal place – a lean-to shelter by a lake, with no other campers there either. Even without a shelter, it is difficult to find a lakeshore – at least in the southern part of the country, almost every lakeshore with a road is occupied by a summer cottage. National parks are troublesome for the reasons mentioned above. There are not many outdoor recreation areas that permit overnight stays, such as Päihäniemi. So I suppose I will have to scout carefully and still be prepared to settle for some turning place on a forest road in the future.


## Equipment

I used the same setup as last time. In the panniers I packed clothes, a sleeping bag, sleeping mat, food and cooking gear. In the top box I put rain gear, gloves and fleece, and the plan was for there to be some space for purchased food and such things. In the tank bag I had water, a coffee thermos, charger, power bank, first-aid kit, cable lock for helmet and jacket, tissues and some snacks. I then put the tent in a waterproof bag which I strapped to the pillion seat, in front of the top box. My slippers also fitted in this bag.

![Packed](hemma_igen.jpg "Just home, with all the luggage still in place.")

Overall, both the setup and the equipment worked really well. The tank bag has extra shoulder straps, so it also functions as a daypack. When needed, it is reasonably easy to carry the panniers over the shoulders for a few hundred metres (in Kolovesi National Park). The top box is also convenient to carry along.

When I parked the motorcycle in a town and wanted to leave the helmet and jacket, I locked them to it with the cable lock. Admittedly, it is mostly a symbolic thing, but at any rate it is not without hassle to take the things when passing by.

I had packed the right amount of clothes and equipment. Of the clothes, only one T-shirt and one pair of socks went unused. And thankfully the rain gear was not needed either.

The recently purchased gas stove came into use and worked excellently. On the last trip I had a spirit stove with me, but I simply could not be bothered to dig one out and fiddle with it. It takes a long time to heat water, and then the spirit must burn out so that it does not leak and so on. The gas stove, on the other hand, is quick, compact and easy to pack away again. I prepared several meals from the freeze-dried food I had brought. It is easy just to heat water. I also made coffee and filled the thermos in the mornings. On the other hand, some of the extra food I had packed (crispbread, soft cheese in a tube, nuts) turned out to be unnecessary. I somehow forgot that I had these provisions.

The only real blunder? I had a bottle of mosquito repellent in the tank bag, and the wretched bottle leaked, with the result that everything in the tank bag began to stink of mosquito repellent. The worst thing is that exactly the same thing happened on the last trip too. If one does not learn from one's mistakes... From now on, it will have to travel in a pannier, where hopefully it will not leak.

## Maintenance

The motorcycle held together. Admittedly, I was worried for a while that there was some problem with the charging. It took a while before anything happened when the start button was pressed. The voltage meter on the USB socket showed rather low readings (< 14 V while riding). I then stopped charging the phone from the USB socket and used the power bank instead. The starting problem became less pronounced. I do not know whether this was just imagination or whether the regulator actually supplies too little power to run the ignition, lighting, Android Auto screen and USB charger. I got home in any case, and when I plugged in the charger it showed nothing unusual.

The left rear indicator malfunctioned again, intermittently. Apparently my repair did not hold. The panniers bend the indicator stalks, so this was probably enough to make the soldering come loose.

One thing that did not get done was lubricating the chain. It should preferably be lubricated every 1,000 km, so I really ought to have done that. The problem is that I keep the lubricant can and other tools under the seat. To get to the can, the seat must come off. And to get at opening the seat, the panniers and tent must come off. Which is, of course, particularly awkward.


## Summary

All in all, a very successful and fairly trouble-free trip. The weather was almost ideal, neither too cold nor too warm. The bike held together. And the old man held up too: sitting in the saddle all day, skipping the occasional meal and sleeping in a tent at night.

![In the tent](nyvaken.jpg "Wake-up at 6 every morning.")

I have neither a sore backside nor any need to stay away from the bike (I actually went on a 114 km short trip to the local shop with the bike today, as enjoyable as ever). So I would gladly do more touring, soon again!
