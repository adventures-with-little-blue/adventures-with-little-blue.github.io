# Changed Chain and Sprockets Again

It almost feels as though it was only the day before yesterday that I [last changed the chain and sprockets](../2025-07-17-drivechain/). Just over a year and 15,485 km later, I have to change them again. There is clattering and rattling, and some of the chain links are quite stiff, though not completely seized.

<!--more-->

First the sprocket cover came off, then it was time to loosen the front sprocket. It is important to do this while the chain is still in place so that the front sprocket can be held steady. A block between the two sides of the swingarm keeps the rear wheel still while turning it.

![Loosening the sprocket](01-front_sprocket.jpg "Loosening the front sprocket.")


Before tackling the chain with the chain breaker, the head of one rivet has to be ground down. This is easiest with the angle grinder and a grinding disc. It happened to have a cutting disc fitted, but that had to do. Once the head of the rivet has been ground down, it should simply be possible to press it out with the chain breaker. Assuming you have a decent kit. I bought a Sealy kit last year. Bloody rubbish. It is so badly designed that the thin pin intended to push out the chain rivet is not held steady sideways, which caused the pin to snap. I eventually managed to press the rivet out with a pin from another chain breaker kit (for ordinary bicycles). I could, of course, simply have cut the chain.

![Grinding the rivet](02-humla.jpg "Ground down a chain rivet with the angle grinder.")

Then off with the rear wheel and on with a new sprocket. The bolts must be tightened to 100 Nm, so it is a little awkward to keep the wheel still.

![New rear sprocket](03-rear_sprocket.jpg "New and shiningly clean rear sprocket.")

When I refitted the rear wheel and it was just hanging from the axle, I noticed that it was quite seriously out of balance. No matter how I spun it, it fairly quickly returned to a position with the valve at about five past twelve. I may have to take the wheel in for balancing; I still want to get another two or three thousand kilometres out of the tyre before replacing it.

![Rear wheel out of balance](04-obalans.jpg "Rear wheel out of balance?")

Then on with the new chain. Once again, it was time to use the awful Sealy kit. When I bought the kit, it said it was for splitting and riveting the chain. The instructions then helpfully state that, when riveting the chain, a special riveting pin must be used (not included). Thanks a lot! This time I pressed hard using one of the small balls included in the kit. I hope that is enough. Last time I used the thinnest pin (which has now broken), and it did at least hold the chain together for its entire service life.

![Joint](05-skarv.jpg "Riveted.")

The old chain and sprockets did not look particularly worn. No shark teeth yet. I do not know why the chain became stiff; I have tried to lubricate it regularly and make sure it does not have time to rust. 15,500 km is not particularly much for a chain on a fairly low-powered motorcycle. The old chain was a JT, but I have now changed to a slightly more expensive D.I.D. Perhaps it resists corrosion better? Or perhaps it will last for the rest of the bike’s life, who knows.

![The old chain and sprockets](06-gammalt_drivpaket.jpg "The old chain and sprockets. They do not look very worn.")

As usual when I am tinkering, things do not exactly go as they do on Strömsö. This time it was because of that awful chain-breaking-and-riveting tool. It was a little past midnight before everything was back together. The test ride therefore did not happen until the following evening. At least there was a big difference in the amount of rattling: it now ran rather quietly and pleasantly. There were also considerably fewer vibrations, though not quite as few as there should be. It now feels mostly like imbalance in the tyres - which is fairly obvious. But perhaps the vibrations I have felt, or am feeling, were caused by just two different things: a stiff chain and an unbalanced rear wheel. Perhaps in combination with a slightly worn steering-head bearing. I have been considering all manner of far more complicated possibilities, such as a cam chain out of sync and worn main bearings. Perhaps it does not need to be so complicated?

The test ride took place in "dry" conditions, but dry conditions in mid-October mean something different from dry conditions in summer. The roads never get completely dry, and dirty water splashes and sprays from the wet patches that constantly remain on the road surface. As a result, Little Blue needed another wash after just 100 km of riding.

![Messy conditions](07-klottigt.jpg "Messy conditions make for splashes.")
